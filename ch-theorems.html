    Chapter 3 Definitions, Theorems and Proofs

    Mathematical writing has its own building blocks. This chapter introduces definitions, theorems, lemmas, corollaries and proofs, then examples and remarks, then cross-references, index entries and citations.
    🔗

    Section 3.1 Definitions, Theorems and Proofs

    Definition 3.1.1 Divisibility.

    Let \(a\) and \(b\) be integers. We say that \(a\) divides \(b\text{,}\) written \(a \divides b\text{,}\) if there is an integer \(k\) with \(b = ak\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 3.1.2 Prime Number.

    An integer \(p \gt 1\) is prime if its only positive divisors are \(1\) and \(p\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Lemma 3.1.3 Divisors of a Sum.

    If \(d \divides a\) and \(d \divides b\text{,}\) then \(d \divides (a+b)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    By Definition 3.1.1 there are integers \(j\) and \(k\) with \(a = dj\) and \(b = dk\text{.}\) Then \(a + b = d(j+k)\text{,}\) and \(j+k\) is an integer.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 3.1.4 Sum of Odd Numbers.

    For every natural number \(n\text{,}\) the sum of the first \(n\) odd numbers is \(n^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    We use induction on \(n\text{.}\) For \(n = 1\) the sum is \(1 = 1^2\text{.}\) Suppose the claim holds for \(n\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 1 + 3 + \cdots + (2n-1) + (2n+1) = n^2 + 2n + 1 = (n+1)^2\text{,} \end{equation*}
    so the claim holds for \(n+1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Corollary 3.1.5.

    The sum of the first ten odd numbers is \(100\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Take \(n = 10\) in Theorem 3.1.4.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Section 3.2 Examples and Remarks

    Example 3.2.1 A Greatest Common Divisor.

    Find the greatest common divisor of \(48\) and \(18\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Repeated division gives
    🔗
    \begin{align*} 48 \amp= 2 \cdot 18 + 12\\ 18 \amp= 1 \cdot 12 + 6\\ 12 \amp= 2 \cdot 6 + 0\text{.} \end{align*}
    The last nonzero remainder is \(6\text{,}\) so \(\gcd(48,18) = 6\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.2.2.

    The number \(91\) is not prime, because \(91 = 7 \cdot 13\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.2.3 On Primes.

    The primes below \(20\) are \(2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 3.2.4 An Even Prime.

    The number \(2\) is the only even prime.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Section 3.3 Cross-References, Index and Citations

    A cross-reference points to anything that has an xml:id. The default text names the target and its number, as in Theorem 3.1.4. You can ask for just the number, as in 3.1.4, or the title, as in Sum of Odd Numbers. A reference to a whole chapter reads Chapter 2, and one to a section reads Section 1.2.
    🔗
    Index entries are invisible in the text. The entry for Euclid’s proof that there are infinitely many primes is cited in [1, Book IX, Proposition 20]. A general introduction to divisibility and primes is [2].
    🔗

    Theorem 3.3.1 Infinitely Many Primes.

    There are infinitely many prime numbers.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose the primes were \(p_1, \dots, p_r\text{.}\) Let \(N = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_r + 1\text{.}\) No \(p_i\) divides \(N\text{,}\) since each leaves remainder \(1\text{.}\) But \(N \gt 1\) has a prime divisor, a contradiction.
